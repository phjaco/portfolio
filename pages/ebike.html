The majority of this project was in the research portion. I spent a year between the summers of '24 and '25 regularly researching methods to the depth of determining the viability of spot welding cells and assembling my own battery.

There is a significant amount of depth to building out an electric bike and you really can start from any point in the process. There are wonderful videos involving assembling your own gearboxes, battery packs, and on occasion custom motor controllers and battery management systems. As a mechanical engineer whose present knowledge of electrical systems did not come naturally, learning electrical system development for a personal project I thought would've been outside the scope I was willing to take on, so the closest to the bottom I was willing to go was at most battery pack development with a stock BMS and motor controller, and the highest was buying a bike outright, as in recent years there has been significant commercial competition in the electric bicycle market.

Note on Legal Considerations

There are additional aspects introduced by this commercialization, primarily increasing government regulation from originally nearly no legislature. These regulations are logical, as there have been an increasing number of lethal incidents regarding these vehicles, and presently many states don't require registration or a driver's license for bikes under 750W. The issue is you cannot determine if a bike is under 750W without stopping the vehicle besides monitoring the speed for the allowed speed limits, which can be mitigated by the user not exceeding standard 28/18mph speed limits applied to the different ebike classes, or by knowing every model of ebike by heart, which is impractical with an increasing number of imported models that exceed legal limits. These laws additionally demonstrate a misunderstanding of how ebikes operate as they are based on the wattage that the motor is rated for, but unlike an engine, a motor can be operated above the wattage it's rated for, can be rated for a wattage lower than it is capable of, and the motor controller can be tweaked in hardware by anyone with aftermarket tools to adjust the limits of the vehicle. In some cases, these bikes are designed to be legal with a single wire made accessible that, when cut, remove the mandated limits from the vehicle. In essence, most bikes are easily capable of exceeding the legal limits imposed on them, and the responsibility lies on the owner to use their vehicle in good faith.

The bike I built is software limited to not exceed the allowed limits when used on public roads and property, and I sought out legislature (VA Code 46.2-100 & 46.2-904.1) to ensure I abide to the law when on public roads. The motor selected's sticker rates it for 250W continuous, but it is physically capable of reaching outputs several times that. I have no intention of using that upper limit unless on private property, and if I want to someday I'd first get the vehicle registered as a moped or motorcycle and acquire an M2 designation to conform to the definitions assigned in 46.2-100. At the core of it, I'm acutely aware of the dangers related to these vehicles for both the rider and others and I always ride responsibly.

The Motor

There are two motor mounting methods for an electric bicycle: mid-drive or hub drive.

Mid-Drive

The mid drive motor is positioned at the crankset, typically with an additional geartrain system. This allows for the motor to have a finer adjusted torque, as you can gear it down, and the center of mass isn't moved longitudinally as the motor sits under the rider. This only lowers the center of mass, which is beneficial for stability anyways.

Hub Drive

Hub motors are mounted in the wheel and provide direct drive. They require completely replacing one or more tires, and they move the center of mass towards the rear of the bike if you only mount one, or keep the center of mass under the rider but drastically increase the mass of the bike on its own. They're generally heavier as well.

Mid-drive motors are superior in every way except for cost, given their complexity.

I decided that I'd want a mid-drive if I could find one for an slightly increased price when compared to a hub, or with a significant power increase. In the end, I selected a CYC Motors X1 Pro 4, a 6000W capable motor and controller with easily the highest power density in a motor I'd seen available, at a price that was ~$1200, controller included. I choose it additionally for the warranty and reliability of the company, as they had several generations of this product and another product competing with typical Bafang and Tongsheng conversion kits in the 750-1500W range, and the power to price ratio was unmatched by any other motor. There was further consideration and research than this abridged version, but the specifications of the CYC Motor were so exceptional I waited an additional few months for corrobative reviews and to see if anything else entered the market on the same level. It is as powerful as claimed.

The Battery

While waiting for more evidence of the motor's specifications, I began researching the battery options. If I wanted to build my own battery, I would need to start from the base by locating reliable cells from a manufacturer, find another way to source high quality cells, or lower my standards of quality. The third option was unacceptable, the first option would likely be too expensive at ~$500 for 72 Samsung 50S 21700s, plus nickel tape, a BMS, the enclosure, and both the time and tools for assembly. This would likely be a few months of ordering, assembly, and ironing out issues for a product that would ultimately be subpar as I have no prior experience. The second option, although more viable, boiled out to likely buying a secondhand battery module for an EV, potentially crashed, salvaging the usable cells, and using those for assembly. It would've saved a few hundred dollars, but all of the cells likely would've seen some use and I wasn't fully comfortable tearing apart a battery module. For me, this meant that building my own battery was not viable.

Given the criteria, I elected to purchase a battery from the same supplier as the motor to keep everything under one provider. This decision ensured compatibility and meant the entire electrical system would be basically designed as one cohesive unit. The battery is a CYC A65, a 65V 20Ah 18s4p pack with Samsung 50S 21700 cells. It came in around $1000, which is likely equivalent to what it would have cost me to build a worse battery from scratch, as it is in a powdercoated aluminum case and I certainly wouldn't have gone to that degree of craftsmanship.

The Issues

Bottom Bracket

When I bought my frame, I bought it secondhand and didn't evaluate the bottom bracket closely, as I was getting a well regarded bike for an exceptionally good price. It turned out that the bottom bracket was PF30, and the supported BBs are BSA 68/73/83mm and BB92. Luckily, BSA is threaded with an OD ~34.8mm, and PF30 has a smooth bore diameter ~46mm, so all I would need is a bottom bracket adapter to convert the larger PF30 down to BSA's size. I purchased an aluminum adapter and discovered that the adapter had a shoulder that was too small for the spindle of the CYC X1 Pro 4 to fit. At this point I had moved back into college and was building it in my livingroom and had already spent a month building and waiting on packages to come in, so I just took it to the machine shop at school and had the ID milled down until it was a light pressfit instead of returning it and finding a new adapter from a different supplier.

The Display

When I received and installed the electric drivetrain, I discovered that it wouldn't turn on and the display would only light momentarily. This meant I had to troubleshoot an electrical system I had never interacted with before, and though at the time I understood the role of every component, I did not know how they were actually connected together. I first tested the battery with a multimeter and confirmed it was putting out 65V, then tested the controller was receiving power and functioning correctly by identifying it briefly putting out a bluetooth signal. To test the display I jumped two of the pinouts with a dupont wire to bypass it in the harness' communication loop, which would identify if the display was the issue if the bike could then turn on. The bike powered on confirming the display was the issue, so the supplier was contacted and I received a replacement display a few weeks later that worked.
