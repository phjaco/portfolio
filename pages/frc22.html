FRC '22: Crystal Mantis

This is being written four years removed, and I will likely omit details because I can't remember them with complete clarity. Dropdown sections will be story-focused, as I think the story of this season is why I'm fond of it, enough so to still include it so many years later.

Brief Overview

The First Robotics Competition is a high school robotics competition where a game is released each year with a 130 page document outlining the rules for both the game and robot's construction. The general size of the robot is a 120 inch perimeter, maximum of ~150 lbs, and maximum height of 7 feet. The robot uses pretty capable motors for driving and actuation, and there are both industry standard drivetrains used as well as a newer one I've only seem in FRC, which I expand on in the appendix. Each year the game is different and the robot must be designed from scratch to fit the game.

My Contribution

My role on the team was as the lead designer for all custom components and maintaining the CAD files. This season we acquird an engraving machine that we used as a lower powered 3 axis CNC mill to manufacture all of our parts, and I played a role in creating the CAM and running the machine as well. This process was individually developed by the students, as no faculty or mentors had prior experience with automated machine work. We performed very basic FEA on our mechanisms to ensure that they could withstand more than the expected stress, and then used shape optimization based on the way stress flows to save weight. As the one designing the CAD, I was the one executing all these processes as well. Almost every part above the frame, excluding bolts and electrical, was drawn by me, had basic simulations performed by me, and had their CAM at the least reviewed by me.

Season's Story & Prior Seasons

'22 was simply the bot that was. With the additional funds from not building a bot during the '21 season we purchased a desktop engraving machine, basically a CNC table with a weaker spindle. The weak spindle meant we were only comfortable cutting easy materials, primarily polycarbonate but up to attempting aluminum. At the time nobody knew how to operate the machine, so I and another student took on the task of figuring out how to take CAD and get a CAM'd part, then how to get that gcode into the machine. We created an operable workflow that we refined out over the course of the season. With this new ability to manufacture custom parts, we started designing things in polycarbonate. And then we designed everything in polycarbonate. The alienoid shape can be attributed to simple shape optimization for weight savings I did in Fusion which I'm not certain was executed correctly. In theory it's a good design feature, and the frame wasn't the part that ever broke. The polycarbonate design was surprisingly impressive as it fell a little under aluminum in strength but cost significantly less than milling aluminum, and the ease of manufacturing let us produce parts very quickly.

The shooter used a camera with fiducial markers to determine distance from the target and adjust the speed, and oriented the robot to align it normal to the line between its center and the target. The climber was a completely unique solution that mimicked how a human would approach monkey bars, using hinged arms to grip the next bar and two rotating arms to hold onto the current one. These arms did not have any stress analysis applied, and did break, but their shape was generic enough that it could be recreated with a jigsaw. Of the many climbers we saw across our competitions, none used a similar design to ours. I choose to believe this was a testament to our creativity rather than the design being overengineered, which it most certainly was.

District Competition

Although we made it to worlds and performed very well, the crowning moment of this season was the final bracket of the District competition. We had one of the hardest brackets statistically assigned in the competition, and as a result were both severely underperforming and underrated. Ranked 32nd of 61 teams, we were not expected to advance to Worlds, and were hoping for a selection from a higher ranking alliance to get a fighting chance. We were the last pick by the 7th alliance of 8, meaning we were the 7th seed. We faced the 2nd seed, then the 3rd seed, and then the 1st seed, only losing in the finals to the 1st seed. But that was good enough, as finalists also progress to Worlds.


I cannot express how miraculous it was we made it so far in the tournament. Our driver was pushed to perform exceptionally well by some external factors. One of our climber's arms had broken and we were all too shaky to cut out a new copy by hand, then a marine veteran unrelated to our team appeared from seemingly thin air and offered to cut it for us because he didn't have shaky hands. Our team thought we had to win it all to go to worlds, and only I and the other captain knew when we qualified prior to the final match that we were going, choosing to withhold that information to keep the driver focused on the finals. This District championship was the highlight of my entire FRC experience.

2019-2021 Seasons

I'm not committing pages to the 2019-2021 seasons as they weren't as successful as the '22 and '23, but I'm including a brief section here to summarize. I started on the team in 2019 as an 8th grader, and we had a very unsuccessful first season; we didn't make it past the initial qualifying competitions, and the robot was designed with too many concepts that weren't integrated well and literally tore it apart. The 2020 season we referred to as "the bot that could've been", as from the gates it was dominating. We'd transitioned to aim being based off fiducial markers, the design was simple and effective, we won the first event as the captain of our alliance, and then the week after that the Pandemic began and the season ended before it could begin. 2021 was still during the pandemic so it was developmental for the team, focused mainly on getting a bot with swerve drive set up.

Appendix A: Drivetrains

There's primarily three drivetrain styles that FRC bots use.

Tank drive, which is typically 3 wheels in a row on both sides with a vertical offset on the center wheels to mitigate bottoming. Tank drive has limited mobility, but is very hard to push around and can defend well in a match.

Mechanum drive uses four wheels with rollers at a ~45 degree angle around the wheel's circumference to create a vectored force when spun that pushes the robot diagonally. By orienting left and right hand wheels mirrored about the center of the robot, you can move in any direction. The downside though is that because the rollers are low friction and the weight on them isn't too significant, they can be pushed around with very little force.

Swerve drive takes the normal wheels of tank drive and places it in an assembly that rotates about the contact patch driven by one motor, and the wheel is powered using a second motor rotating a mitre gear and mitred ring gear on the wheel routed through that rotation assembly. It's a complicated assembly with a lot of parts, but it can move in any direction and isn't easily pushed around. The complicated assembly and doubled motors does mean it is the most expensive option.

Swerve drive seems to perform as the best drivetrain, and prior to the 2020 seasons barely existed beyond barebone modules from kit companys. One manufacturer who was involved with a well regarded team worked on developing a swerve drive system and began producing it themselves in small batch quantities around 2021, and and we managed to obtain a few of these modules. Of the 32 teams that made it to the finals in '23, something like 25 of them used swerve drive, and rules were being written around these drivetrain styles to limit dual motor drive setups to keep competition fair for teams without the funds to get these modules.

Swerve drive is also interesting in that I think on a conceptual level it mirrors how "crab walk" features on some modern luxury vehicles operate, so as a technology it's not just limited to FRC. Similarly, I've seen firsthand application of mechanum drive in some jobs I've held for mobile operation of massive skids, and tank drive has been a classic drivetrain style for decades.
