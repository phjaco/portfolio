Eddy Current Damping

The concept at work in this experiment is conductive materials that aren't ferromagnetic do not exhibit attraction when placed in a magnetic field, but when you move a magnet within range of the material the magnetic field induces a current in the material. This eddy current then generates its own magnetic field that opposes the original, resisting the motion of the magnet.

Controlling the distance of the magnet from the conductive material controls the strength of the magnetic field it feels, the current produced, and the opposing force created. If it is attached to an oscillating spring-mass system, it can be interpretted as a variable damper, and the goal of the experiment was to examine the relationship between distance and damping ratio to see if additional variables such as the mass or spring constants of the system impacts the damping ratio as the math would predict. We did this by first collecting enough data to create predictive models to compare our further, alternative tests against.

My role in this experiment was taking part in the original ideation and research, because we had enough of an understanding of the electricity and magnetism effects at work to come up with the idea, but not great enough to organize the experiment off our knowledge alone. This led me to one paper in particular from the 80s that orchestrated several similar setups to ours, though not exactly the same. The most interesting thing I found was that the relationship between the induced magnetic field and distance of the original magnet from the object should change as it becomes closer, because at a further distance it can be treated as a dipole, but as it gets closer it acts more as a monopole, and when extremely close can be treated as an infinitely extended wire, reducing it from '$B \propto \frac{1}{r^3}$' to '$B \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$' or '$B \propto \frac{1}{r}$'. For reference, the opposing force's relationship with magnetic field should be '$F \propto B^2$', so it should have had a noticable affect on our data.

Damping ratio was approximated using the log decrement by comparing two successive peaks after early attempts to approximate it from the traditional analysis and the original equation. It was then translated to the further tests by using the typical damping ratio equation to create ratios from the mass components that our original could then be multiplied by. This new list of damping ratios were then be compared against the experimental damping ratios found with the new masses.

Comparison of damping ratios against the height of the magnetic damper from the rail.

Comparison of Predicted vs. Experimental Damping Ratio along a 1:1 line.

The data was fairly consistent with what we predicted, and we reasoned that the error we did have could be attributed to a larger frictional force on our cart's wheels when more mass was added. It was an interesting experiment but it was a little lackluster, as it focused more on confirming something we knew was almost definitely true.
